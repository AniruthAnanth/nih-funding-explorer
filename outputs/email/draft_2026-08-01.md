Subject: RE: MGB Department of Surgery — how we rank on NIH funding

Hi Dr. Yang,

Two things up front on what is actually answerable. Department-level **revenue**
is not public — neither hospitals nor medical schools report it at that grain —
so a revenue ranking cannot be built from any source I can find. What is public
and complete is **NIH funding**, so I built the analysis on NIH's ExPORTER files
for FY2021–FY2025.

The complication is that NIH assigns a department code only to universities, not
to independent hospitals. MGB is therefore **absent from every published NIH
departmental ranking** — not ranked low, simply missing, along with Vanderbilt,
Mayo, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Boston Children's and Dana-Farber. To fix that I
rebuilt each investigator's department from their dated PubMed affiliations, and
validated the rule against NIH's own department field at the institutions where
both exist (Cohen's κ 0.92 on a 260-investigator sample).

What it shows:

- **MGB is the largest NIH recipient in the country** — $5.11B over FY2021–FY2025
  and $1.05B in FY2025, ahead of Johns Hopkins ($4.13B and $845M) in every one of
  the five years.

- **MGB Department of Surgery: $266.0M**, across 364 award-years, 134 distinct
  projects and **179 R01 award-years**. That is ahead of Duke ($220.8M), the
  largest department of surgery NIH itself codes, then WashU ($154.1M) and
  Michigan ($131.7M) — and ahead of every hospital department reconstructed so
  far, the closest being Memorial Sloan Kettering at $91.3M. Worth knowing that
  this is MGH and BWH combined, since they remain separate departments: alone
  they are $160.7M and $105.3M.

- **M awards no longer exist.** No M-series award was made anywhere in the
  country in FY2021–FY2025. M01 general clinical research centres were retired
  into the CTSA programme, whose successors are U-series, so any comparison on
  "R01 and M awards" is really a comparison on R01s.

- **Across specialties**, MGB's Department of Medicine is **$1.66B** over the same
  period — larger than any NIH-coded department of medicine in the country (NYU
  Grossman $1.27B, UCSF $1.16B). Nationally, surgery is the 19th largest
  department of the 44 NIH names, and departments of medicine are roughly ten
  times larger than departments of surgery. A surgery-to-medicine comparison is
  measuring a structural difference in how NIH funds research, not a difference
  in performance.

On the international question, NIH data cannot answer it. Non-US organizations
received **0.69%** of all NIH funding over the period, and China has five NIH
recipients totalling $5.9M — a Shanghai department could be the largest in the
world and would be invisible here. I am still looking for a source that supports
a real international comparison and will follow up separately.
