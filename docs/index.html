Read this before reading any departmental ranking

NIH publishes a department code only for recipients it classifies as schools. Independent hospitals and research institutes get none. In FY2025 that leaves 30.0% of all US NIH dollars with no usable department attached, which is $10.5B.

Counted the wide way: no ORG_DEPT value at all, plus the placeholder values NONE, MISCELLANEOUS and NO CODE ASSIGNED, none of which name a department. Counting only the entirely absent ones gives 25.2% and $8.84B. Both are published; the wide definition is used here because a placeholder is no more rankable than a blank.

A departmental ranking taken straight from NIH data therefore does not rank those recipients low, it omits them entirely. Of the 140 US institutions above $50M in FY2025, 27 carry no department code on any of their funding — 25 under the narrow definition — among them Massachusetts General Hospital, Vanderbilt University Medical Center, Brigham and Women's Hospital, Fred Hutchinson, Mayo, Boston Children's, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Dana-Farber and Cedars-Sinai.

Where a department has been reconstructed from dated publication affiliations instead, the row is hatched and tagged lower bound. Those figures are not measured the same way as an NIH-coded department and should not be read as if they were. The rule that produces them is scored against NIH's own field in docs/validation: Cohen's κ 0.916 on a 260-investigator sample. The Method agreement tab scores a different and superseded rule, and reports a much lower κ for that reason.

Two limits on that κ, stated because they are easy to miss. The sample is 1:1 case-control — 150 surgical against 150 non-surgical — so its 50% prevalence is not the population's 7%; the reported precision of 100% carries a 95% upper bound on the false-positive rate of about 2.4%, which at a 7% base rate admits a true precision nearer 75%. And the sample can only be drawn where NIH publishes a department, meaning at universities, while the rule is applied at hospitals. The transfer from one to the other is the whole point of the method and is the one thing the experiment cannot measure.

The NIH-coded side of this site agrees with the published benchmark, row by row. The Blue Ridge Institute for Medical Research compiles the reference NIH departmental rankings from the same year-end data. Matched institution by institution for FY2025 departments of surgery, 55 of 74 agree to the dollar as awarded — Duke, WashU, UCSF, Stanford, Michigan, Minnesota, Johns Hopkins, Columbia, Weill Cornell, UNC, Rutgers, Indiana, UT Southwestern and UCLA among them. Applying BRIMR's own conventions for mid-year institution transfers and academic affiliates takes that to 57 of 74 and cuts the total residual from $23.5M to $10.2M. Every institution and the reason for every remaining difference is published as external_benchmark_brimr_surgery_2025.csv; twelve are still unexplained and are labelled as such rather than smoothed over.

Two differences are structural, and the second is the more interesting. BRIMR's surgery table lists 75 institutions and not one of them is a hospital — the coverage gap on this page, confirmed from the outside. And BRIMR attributes Vanderbilt University Medical Center's surgery to "Vanderbilt University": VUMC is 524 awards and $451.6M in FY2025 carrying no department code at all. BRIMR reports $14,408,389 of Vanderbilt surgery; NIH codes $336,630 of it at the university; the reconstruction adds $10,858,628 at VUMC from publication affiliations alone. Coded plus reconstructed is 77.7% of BRIMR's figure. That is the only place an outside number exists to calibrate the reconstruction against, and a method described as a lower bound recovering roughly three quarters of an independently compiled total is behaving as described.

A second calibration, from the department itself. MGH's Department of Surgery publishes its own annual figures: $35M of NIH expenditures in 2024, against $74M of total research expenditures. The reconstruction here gives $30.75M of NIH awards for FY2024 — 87.9%, having used nothing but PubMed affiliations. Vanderbilt gave 77.7% on the comparison below. Two hospitals, two unrelated sources, both in the high seventies to high eighties, and both short. That is the claim these figures carry.

That same slide is the sharpest available statement of what an NIH ranking is not. NIH is 47% of what that department spends on research. The other $39M — Department of Defense, foundations, industry, philanthropy, royalties — appears nowhere in this dataset or in any NIH-derived ranking, including this one.

Reconstruction is applied to every uncoded recipient in the comparison set, not to a chosen few. Rebuilding one hospital's departments and leaving its uncoded peers out does not correct NIH's coverage gap; it inverts it, and leaves that hospital ranked against a field with the other hospitals deleted from it.

Surgery against every other specialty

Pooled across all institutions NIH codes, surgery is the 19th largest department nationally over FY2021–FY2025, at $2.56B, or 1.37% of department-coded NIH funding. Internal medicine is roughly an order of magnitude larger.

Against every institution–department pair in the country:

  • The best-funded NIH-coded surgery department in the US ranks 67th overall over five years (Duke, $221M), and 63rd in FY2025.
  • Exactly one surgery department appears in the national top 100. Thirteen appear in the top 500.
  • NYU's Department of Medicine alone took $1.27B over five years — about 5.7× the entire Duke Department of Surgery, the largest NIH-coded surgery department in the country.

This is a structural feature of how NIH funds research, not a judgment about surgical science. Departments of medicine are far larger and NIH's disease-oriented institutes fund them accordingly. Comparing a surgery department to a medicine department compares a department to something about ten times its size.

How the two methods compare

These tables score a rule this site no longer uses. They measure a first attempt that matched awards to surgical papers institution by institution, from a PubMed harvest already filtered to surgical topics. Scored against NIH's own field it reached Cohen's κ of about 0.27, and that is why it was replaced. The rule the rankings actually use classifies each investigator from an unfiltered pull of their papers and reaches κ 0.916 on a 260-investigator sample; its validation is in docs/validation/, not here. Two very different κ values appear on this site and they belong to two different rules.

The comparison is kept published because the gap is the argument for the change, and because the underlying asymmetry it exposes is real: NIH's ORG_DEPT exists only for recipients it classifies as schools, so any method resting on it measures hospitals and universities by different rules and silently drops the hospitals.

  • Sensitivity — of the award-years NIH calls surgical, the share the publication method also calls surgical. This is the number that says how much a publication-derived total undercounts.
  • Precision — of the award-years the publication method calls surgical, the share NIH agrees with.
  • Cohen's κ — chance-corrected agreement on the surgical / not-surgical call.

An institution NIH does not department-code contributes no comparison at all. Those are listed separately rather than dropped, because their absence from the NIH column is the finding.

Method & data

What this data is

NIH ExPORTER annual project files, FY2021–FY2025, downloaded and checksummed 1 August 2026. One row per APPLICATION_ID — an award-year. Both award-years and distinct core projects are reported everywhere, so continuation years are never read as new grants.

What is excluded, and why

  • Non-NIH agencies. ExPORTER also carries VA, AHRQ, CDC, FDA and HRSA awards. Only the 27 NIH institutes and centers are retained.
  • Intramural research and contracts. Grants and cooperative agreements only.
  • Subproject rows. They sit inside a parent award that already carries the full TOTAL_COST; keeping both would double count.

Three things this data cannot do

  • It is not revenue. NIH obligations only — no philanthropy, industry, foundation, DoD, institutional or clinical revenue. A department's NIH total is usually a minority of its research revenue.
  • It is not a world ranking. Non-US organizations received 0.69% of NIH funding over FY2021–FY2025. China has five NIH recipients totalling $5.9M across the period; Japan one at $0.1M; South Korea one at $54,000; Singapore none. A department in Shanghai could be the largest in the world and would be invisible here.
  • There are no M awards to count. M-series activity codes return zero award-years for every institution in FY2021–FY2025. NIH retired the M01 General Clinical Research Center mechanism into the CTSA program; the successors are UL1 and UM1, classified here under the U family.

How MGH and BWH surgical figures were reconstructed

  1. An affiliation string must name both the institution and a surgical unit, adjacent to each other within the same segment of the string.
  2. Composite “From the …” affiliation blocks, which PubMed attaches wholesale to every author, are rejected — 7,375 of them.
  3. The author must match an NIH contact PI on surname plus first initial at the same institution. Any surname/initial covering more than one forename is flagged ambiguous and dropped.
  4. Evidence must fall within three years of the award's index date.
  5. Everything unmatched stays unknown. Nothing is imputed.

Only 7.0% of MGH/BWH contact-PI award-years resolve to a surgical department this way, which is why the result is a lower bound rather than an estimate. Publication-based reconstruction misses anyone who does not publish with a departmental affiliation string.
