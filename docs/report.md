# NIH funding by institution and department, FY2021–FY2025

**1 August 2026.** Source: NIH ExPORTER annual project files FY2021–FY2025,
downloaded and checksummed 1 August 2026. Every number below is regenerated by
`python3 run_pipeline.py all`, after the two cached stages that sit outside it
have been run once: `harvest` for the PubMed affiliations and `profile` for the
per-investigator department profiles. Tables are written to `outputs/tables/`,
figures to `outputs/figures/`.

## The short version

This is a reproducible census of every NIH extramural grant and cooperative
agreement awarded FY2021–FY2025, ranked three ways: by recipient organization,
by department, and by institution–department pair. 315,717 award-years across
5,007 organizations and 9,683 institution–department pairs.

**Recipient rankings are straightforward.** NIH names the recipient on every
award, so ranking organizations by dollars, award-years, distinct projects and
R01s needs no inference. Section 1 has the table.

**Departmental rankings are not.** NIH publishes a department code only for
recipients it classifies as schools. Independent hospitals and research
institutes get none, which in FY2025 leaves 30.0% of US NIH dollars — $10.5B —
with no usable department attached. That is the wide definition: the field is
absent (`__MISSING__`) or carries one of the three placeholders NIH writes in
place of a department, `NONE`, `MISCELLANEOUS` or `NO CODE ASSIGNED`. Counting
`__MISSING__` alone it is 25.2%, $8.84B. Of the 140 US institutions above $50M
that year, 27 carry no usable department code on any of their funding, or 25 on
the `__MISSING__`-only definition: Massachusetts General Hospital, Vanderbilt
University Medical Center, Brigham and Women's Hospital, Fred Hutchinson, Mayo,
Boston Children's, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Children's Hospital of
Philadelphia, Cincinnati Children's, Beth Israel Deaconess, Dana-Farber and
Cedars-Sinai among them. A departmental ranking built on that field does not
rank those recipients low. It omits them, and their absence is a coding artifact
rather than a result.

The response is to derive every institution's departments the same way, from
dated publication affiliations, so that hospitals and universities are measured
by one rule. Section 3 describes the method and section 3.1 reports how far the
institution-level publication matcher agrees with NIH's own field, which is not
very far: sensitivity 33.6%, precision 30.7%, Cohen's κ 0.267. Two different
κ values live in this repository and belong to two different rules. κ 0.267 is
the institution-level matcher measured in section 3.1. κ 0.916 is the
per-investigator majority rule that produces the reconstructed departments in
section 5, measured on a separate 300-PI sample. Neither number licenses the
other. Publication-derived departmental figures are published as **lower
bounds** and marked as such wherever they appear.

**A second measure runs alongside the funding tables.** Section 8 counts
publications in NEJM, JAMA, BMJ and Lancet by each institution's surgical
faculty, together with total citations and field-normalised RCR from NIH's
iCite. It needs no department field from NIH and no award attribution, so it is
like-for-like across the 32 harvested institutions in a way the funding
attribution is not. Those 32 are not a national field, and section 8 says who
is missing from them.

**Two questions this data cannot answer.** There is no world ranking in it:
non-US organizations received 0.69% of NIH funding over five years, and China's
five NIH recipients total $5.9M between them. And there are no M-series awards
to count, because NIH retired the M01 mechanism into the CTSA program. Section 7
covers both.

## 3. How a department is measured here, and why it changed

The obvious approach is to take NIH's department field and rank on it. That was
built first and then dropped as the primary measure, for one reason: it
does not exist for hospitals. Ranking Duke's Department of Surgery by NIH's own
field against MGB's Department of Surgery by anything else is not a comparison.
It measures two institutions with two instruments and reports the difference as
though it were a fact about the institutions.

So the primary measurement is now the same for everyone. For each of the **32
institutions harvested from the 57 in `reference/pubmed_institutions_v1.csv`**
the pipeline harvests
dated author affiliation strings from PubMed, decides which of them describe a
surgical appointment, matches those authors to NIH contact PIs, and credits an
award to the surgical department when the evidence sits near the award's index
date. The chain is identical for MGH and for Duke. That is the entire point of
running it this way.

Five guards, each of which costs recall on purpose:

1. the affiliation string must name **both** the institution and a surgical
   unit, adjacent to each other inside the same segment of the string;
2. composite "From the …" affiliation blocks, which some journals print once and
   PubMed then attaches wholesale to every author on the paper, are rejected
   outright. 7,375 of them so far;
3. the author must match an NIH contact PI on surname plus first initial at the
   same institution. Any surname and initial covering more than one forename at
   that institution is ambiguous and is dropped rather than guessed;
4. the evidence must fall within three years of the award's index date;
5. anything unmatched stays **unknown**. Nothing is imputed.

One pattern in `reference/department_string_patterns_v1.csv` has to be named
here, because a headline figure leans on it. Priority 99 is a bare `\bsurg`
mapping to `OTHER_EXPLICIT_SURGERY`, which sits inside the Department of Surgery
set. It is ranked last on purpose, so any named department beats it, but it
remains the weakest string in the crosswalk and it is not carrying a trivial
load: `OTHER_EXPLICIT_SURGERY` accounts for **12.6% of the current reconstructed
MGB Department of Surgery total**, about an eighth of it, across 12
investigators and 60 award-years. Roughly two thirds of the distinct MGH and
BWH affiliation strings that land in that bucket were matched by the bare
catch-all rather than by the named "department of surgery" pattern ranked above
it. The rest of the evidence base is not thin in the same way: the median
investigator in the surgical set contributes 22 classified affiliation strings,
and only 2 of the 80 were decided on a single string, worth $1.7M between them.

**One confidence floor is published, not two.** An earlier build shipped
`corroborated` alongside an `all_evidence` floor and called the gap between them
an uncertainty band. It was not one. The second floor selected the identical set
of investigators, the two CSVs were byte-identical, the site control that
switched between them changed nothing, and it carried the validation statistics
of a rule that had not produced it. The `all_evidence` outputs have been
deleted. `corroborated` survives as a filename token so existing links and
downloads keep working, not as one end of a range.

The 32 institutions were chosen to cover the comparison rather than sampled.
Every one of the top 20 NIH-coded departments of surgery is among them, and
together the 32 hold **80.6% of all NIH surgery-department dollars** over five
years, $2.06B of $2.56B. That selection rule is keyed on NIH's department code,
which is the artifact this analysis exists to correct, so the comparison set
carries the coding bias forward. Section 8 says who that leaves out.

### NIH's own field becomes the check, not the measure

Demoting `ORG_DEPT` to a comparator makes it useful in a way it was not before.
On the subset of award-years where NIH supplies a department,
`src/rankmgb/agreement.py` reports how often the publication method agrees with
it: sensitivity (of the awards NIH calls surgical, the share the publication
method also catches, which is a direct measure of how badly a publication-derived
total undercounts), precision (of the awards the publication method calls
surgical, the share NIH agrees with), and Cohen's κ for chance-corrected
agreement. Institutions NIH does not code have no label to compare against, so
they are listed separately as uncomparable rather than quietly dropped.

`python3 run_pipeline.py surgery` writes three tables.
`agreement_overall.csv` is one row: on the **116,660 contact-PI award-years
where NIH supplies a department**, NIH calls 8,229 surgical and the publication
matcher calls 9,016 surgical, agreeing on 2,766. That is sensitivity 33.6%,
precision 30.7%, raw agreement 90.0% and Cohen's κ 0.267.
`agreement_by_institution.csv` repeats it for each of the 30 comparable
institutions, from κ 0.459 at UC Davis down to κ 0.030 at Columbia, where the
matcher calls 1,143 award-years surgical against NIH's 174.
`agreement_uncomparable.csv` counts, for each of the 32 harvested institutions,
the award-years that carry no NIH label to compare against: 5,142 at MGH and
3,222 at BWH, which is all of theirs, and a residue at every coded university
too, 621 at Michigan and 370 at UCLA. They are listed rather than dropped
because "no comparison is possible here" is itself the finding.

**Those figures score the superseded rule, not the shipped one.** What
`agreement.py` measures is the institution-level publication matcher described
above: an award is called surgical when a surgical affiliation string for that
PI at that institution sits near the award's index date. The reconstructed
departments published in section 5 no longer come from that matcher. They come
from the per-investigator majority rule in `src/rankmgb/pi_department.py`, which
pulls every paper for one author at one institution with no topic filter and
takes the department holding a majority of that author's own classified
affiliation strings. Scored on its own 300-PI sample it reaches **κ 0.916,
sensitivity 91.9%, precision 100.0%**. So: κ 0.267 is the institution-level
matcher, κ 0.916 is the per-investigator majority rule, and quoting either in
support of the other is an error.

Three limits on what κ 0.916 licenses, all of them consequences of how the
sample was drawn rather than of the rule itself.

**The sample is 1:1 case-control and the statistics are prevalence-dependent.**
`docs/validation/validate_department_rule.py` draws 150 surgical and 150
non-surgical contact PIs, so surgical prevalence in the sample is 50%. In the
population it is 7%: 1,861 surgical of the 26,232 contact PIs the sample is
drawn from, or 8,229 of 116,660 counted in award-years, which is the figure
`agreement_overall.csv` carries. Precision moves with prevalence and does not
transfer between the two. The measured precision is
100.0% because the rule produced zero false positives on the 124 resolved
non-surgical PIs in the sample, but zero of 124 puts the 95% upper bound on the
false-positive rate at about 2.4%, and a 2.4% false-positive rate against a 7%
base rate admits a precision near 75%. Sensitivity and κ are estimated on the
same artificial mix. Read 100.0% as "no false positive was observed in 124
tries", not as a population precision.

**The sample is drawn from universities and used to license the rule at
hospitals.** Sampling requires an NIH department to score against, so every one
of the 300 PIs sits at an institution NIH department-codes. The rule is then
applied at institutions NIH does not code. The gap between those two populations
is the exact gap the method exists to bridge, and it is the one thing the
validation cannot measure.

**One binary outcome was scored: surgical or not.** Nothing in the 300-PI sample
tests whether a PI the rule labels `INTERNAL_MEDICINE` is really in a department
of medicine, or `PEDIATRICS` in a department of pediatrics. Every non-surgical
reconstructed department, including everything in `mgb_departments_all.csv`,
rests on an unvalidated extension of a rule validated on one binary call. Those
figures also come from a weaker computation than the validated one:
`pi_department.summarise_all_departments` selects on `modal_department.notna()`,
which is the investigator's plurality department, whereas the validated rule
requires an outright majority. Its docstring claims "the same validated majority
rule" and that claim is wrong. Modal and majority scored within noise of each
other on the surgical call (κ 0.923 against 0.916), which is the only evidence
there is that the substitution is harmless.

The comparison set is now the whole field. Every uncoded recipient in it — 27

The peers came in lower than their total NIH funding would suggest, which is
worth stating plainly because it is what the ranking rests on. Vanderbilt
University Medical Center takes $2.08B of NIH funding in total and returns
$36.7M of Department of Surgery; Mayo Clinic Rochester takes $1.33B and returns
$13.0M; Cincinnati Children's takes $823M and returns $9.2M. Those are real
measurements of surgical research funding, not of surgical volume or of the
institutions.

An earlier version of this table reported MGB 4th of 96 at $126M, from the
superseded per-award matcher, against a field its uncoded peers were absent
from. Both halves of that were wrong in the same direction and they did not
cancel: the rule undercounted MGB, and the field flattered it.

The like-for-like table from the superseded matcher is
`surgery_ranking_{period}_corroborated.csv`, carrying a
`nih_vs_publication_ratio` column that shows, per institution, how far the two
measurements sit apart wherever both exist. It is kept under `outputs/tables/`
and is deliberately not mirrored into `docs/tables/`, because it puts Duke's
department of surgery at $427M where NIH's own field codes $221M, a
`nih_vs_publication_ratio` of 0.52. That matcher misses two thirds of what NIH
calls surgical and calls surgical a great deal NIH does not, and on dollars the
second error is the larger one.

What the evidence supports. The reconstructed figures remain **lower bounds**
and the NIH-coded ones do not, so the two tiers are still not measured
identically and the table says which is which on every row. The gap that
qualified the ordinal before — a field with the hospitals deleted from it — is
closed. What has not been done is the faculty-roster check in section 10, which
is what would turn a lower bound into a count.

Why $266.0M is low rather than merely uncertain. Publication reconstruction misses
anyone who does not publish with a departmental affiliation string. That
disproportionately means basic scientists holding appointments in surgical
departments, and investigators at institutions whose style guide standardises
affiliations to the hospital name alone. Of the 8,446 **MGH and BWH**
contact-PI award-years, the per-investigator profiling currently resolves 7,404
to a department of any kind, 87.7%; the remaining 1,042 are published as
unknown, not as non-surgical, and none of them can contribute to a surgical
total. That coverage figure is recomputed on every run and written to the run
log as it goes — `logs/pipeline.log`, logger `pi_department` — so read it from
there rather than from this sentence.

## 7. What this data cannot do

Two of these, the world comparison and the M awards, are common requests.
The third is a framing point that matters if this report gets quoted alongside
departmental revenue figures.

**NIH funding is not revenue.** Everything here is NIH obligations. No
philanthropy, industry, foundation, DoD, PCORI or AHRQ money, no institutional
support, no clinical revenue. A department's NIH total is usually a minority of
its research revenue and a small fraction of its total revenue. If revenue is
the real question, this dataset answers a different one.

**There is no world ranking in NIH data.** Non-US organizations received **0.69%
of NIH funding** over five years, $1.21B of $174B. The largest non-US recipient
is the University of Cape Town at $73M, which reflects NIH global-health
programs rather than institutional research scale. **China has five NIH
recipients totalling $5.9M** across the period. Japan has one at $0.1M, Korea one
at $54K, Singapore none. A Shanghai department of medicine could be the largest
on earth and would be invisible here. Ranking MGB against it needs a different
source: OpenAlex or Dimensions for publication and grant output, or national
funder databases.

**There are no M awards to count.** M-series activity codes return **zero
award-years for every institution in FY2021–FY2025**. NIH retired the M01
General Clinical Research Center mechanism and folded it into the CTSA program.
The successors are UL1 and UM1, which this pipeline classifies under the U
family. For the large center-type awards, the U and P family columns in every
table are the ones to read. For career development,
that is the K family, in the same tables.

## 8. A second measure: publications and citation impact

`outputs/tables/bibliometrics_surgery.csv`

NIH dollars measure one thing. This measures another, and it has a property the
funding attribution lacks: it needs no NIH department field and no award
attribution, so it is measured the same way everywhere it is measured at all.

Where it is not measured at all matters more. The table is 33 rows per period:
the 32 harvested institutions plus the MGB_CORE roll-up over MGH and BWH. Those
32 were selected as the top 20 NIH-coded departments of surgery and their near
neighbours, which keys the comparison set on the very coding artifact this
report exists to correct. Mayo, Vanderbilt, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Boston
Children's, Fred Hutchinson, Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Dana-Farber
and Cedars-Sinai are absent from it because NIH assigns them no department to
rank in the first place. Five more of the 30 largest US recipients over five
years are absent as well: University of Washington at $2.71B, UNC Chapel Hill
at $2.64B, Mount Sinai at $2.22B, USC at $1.70B and OHSU at $1.44B. Nothing
below is a national ranking, and every "leads" or "first" in this section means
first among these 32.

A paper counts for an institution's surgical department when at least one
author's own affiliation string names both that institution and a surgical unit,
adjacent to each other in the same segment. Journal, publication year, citation
count and Relative Citation Ratio come from NIH's iCite. 129,304 distinct papers
across the 32 institutions carry a surgical affiliation across the 2020–2026
harvest window, 156,975 institution–paper pairs (`logs/biblio.log`). The table
below is then filtered to the reporting period, so its paper counts are smaller
than that.

Top of the table by output in the four top-tier general medical journals:

| Institution | Papers | NEJM+JAMA+BMJ+Lancet | NEJM | JAMA | Total citations | Citations/paper | Mean RCR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass General Brigham | 9,572 | 53 | 7 | 25 | 130,345 | 13.6 | 2.24 |
| Michigan | 5,135 | 26 | 1 | 19 | 69,200 | 13.5 | 2.25 |
| Duke | 6,033 | 21 | 1 | 10 | 69,940 | 11.6 | 2.10 |
| Johns Hopkins | 6,586 | 20 | 0 | 15 | 79,811 | 12.1 | 2.08 |
| Weill Cornell | 3,678 | 17 | 1 | 9 | 53,472 | 14.5 | 2.34 |
| MD Anderson | 3,243 | 12 | 0 | 6 | 67,332 | 20.8 | 2.56 |

Three things to read carefully.

Volume and intensity separate. The leader on absolute output and total citations
is mid-pack on citations per paper and on RCR, where MD Anderson leads at 20.8
and 2.56. Absolute totals partly measure how large a department is; RCR is
field- and time-normalised and is the fairer cross-department comparison.

Roll-ups are unions, not sums. The Mass General Brigham figure deduplicates the
**463 papers** over FY2021–FY2025 carrying surgical authors at both member
hospitals, which a naive sum would double count along with their citations. The
594 figure in `logs/pipeline.log` is the same overlap taken across the whole
2020–2026 harvest window rather than across the reporting period. That
deduplication is also the only reason the top line of the table is a Mass
General Brigham line: **MGB_CORE leads on total citations only as a merged
entity.** Reported separately over five years neither member leads. MGH has
68,561 citations and BWH 68,131, and Johns Hopkins is first among these 32 at
79,811.

Participation counts do not add up to a national total. A paper with surgical
authors at two institutions counts once for each, by design.

Citations accumulate with age, so a five-year window favours its earlier years.
That is the reason RCR is reported alongside the raw count rather than instead
of it.

## 9. Reproducibility and known defects

- **315,717 award-years** FY2021–FY2025 after inclusion rules, from five
  checksummed source files. US annual totals range $33.2B to $35.4B.
- **17 of 17 validation checks pass** (`outputs/tables/validation_report.csv`),
  including a RePORTER cross-check, MGH and BWH separation, no Harvard
  reassignment, and no organization-name collisions.
- The pipeline fails loudly on schema drift, implausible record counts and
  unmapped activity codes. It caught two real problems during this build: an NIH
  schema rename in FY2025, where `CFDA_CODE` became
  `ASSISTANCE_LISTING_NUMBER`, and one application published in two annual files
  with different amounts, resolved by a documented rule and logged in
  `logs/duplicate_application_resolution.csv`.
- Two classification bugs were found by audit and fixed, with regression tests
  in `tests/test_patterns.py`. The urology pattern read `urolog`, which also
  matches "ne**urolog**y"; three quarters of its hits were Department of
  Neurology strings. And composite "From the …" affiliation blocks were
  crediting one author's department to every co-author on the paper. Both fixes
  moved the reconstructed numbers down, which is the direction an audit should move
  things.

Everything unresolved is published as unresolved. Coverage rates are in
`coverage_department_evidence.csv`. The audit trail that ships is
`reconstructed_department_evidence.csv`, one row per reconstructed surgical
award-year, written by `pi_department.emit_evidence`. **It is per investigator,
not per PMID.** The majority rule decides a person rather than an award, so what
each row carries is how many of that investigator's dated affiliation strings
were classified, what share of them were surgical, which department held the
majority and how the name was matched. No award here is attributed by a PMID and
the file does not claim one. The superseded per-award file that did carry a PMID
and a verbatim affiliation string on every row,
`mgb_surgical_award_years_evidence.csv`, is no longer mirrored into
`docs/tables/`; it stays under `outputs/tables/` so the change of method remains
reproducible rather than merely asserted.

## 10. What would close the remaining gap

The MGB surgical figure is a lower bound because only tiers A, B and F of the
evidence hierarchy in `docs/annotation_manual.md` are built. Tier F is the
publication layer described in section 3. Tiers C, D and E are dated
institutional faculty rosters, archived snapshots of those rosters, and ORCID or
biosketch employment history. Together they would turn the lower bound into a
defensible point estimate.

Concretely, for MGH and BWH:

1. Capture the current MGH and BWH Department of Surgery faculty rosters, plus
   Wayback snapshots back to 2020, so a historical appointment is dated rather
   than assumed from today's roster. The manual forbids using a current page to
   assign a 2021 affiliation, and that rule should stay.
2. Match the roster against the **1,778 MGH and BWH contact-PI records**
   already profiled in `data/processed/pi_departments.parquet` — 1,101 at MGH
   and 677 at BWH, which is 1,761 distinct people once the 17 who hold awards at
   both hospitals are counted once — or 2,386 investigator–institution records
   counting every named role. Roster
   matching is much higher-yield than publication matching because it reaches
   non-publishing and basic-science faculty, which is exactly the population the
   publication method loses.
3. Have two annotators independently code a stratified sample and report
   inter-rater agreement, per section 10 of the annotation manual.

That is roughly a week of work. It would let the MGB number be published without
the lower-bound qualifier. The schema, the annotation manual, the taxonomy and
the override mechanism are all built and waiting for the records.
